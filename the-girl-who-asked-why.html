The Girl Who Asked Why

a moonlit story for older hearts
· · ·

In 2041, the old factory lot had been empty for thirty years.

Ines knew this because her grandmother told her, and her grandmother told her everything, whether Ines asked or not.

"That was the Jensen works," her grandmother would say, pointing at the field from the window of the tram. "My father worked there. Twelve hours a day, six days a week, and he was grateful for it. Can you imagine? Grateful."

Ines could not imagine. She had grown up in a world where the floor beneath everyone was solid — housing, food, medicine, connection — and she had never once had to be grateful for it. It was simply there, the way air was there, and she had never thought to ask what it cost.

She was sixteen years old and she did not know what she was for.

This was not a crisis. Nobody in her town treated it as a crisis. Her mother said, "You have time." Her teachers said, "You will find it." Her friends said, "Come to the League with us, it'll be fun," and Ines said, "Fine," because she had nothing better to do.

· · ·

The League was ridiculous. Everyone knew it was ridiculous. It was a competition between towns, played out over a year, in eight categories that had been chosen by committee and were, by any sensible measure, absurd: growing, building, cooking, repairing, storytelling, music, engineering, and something called rapid response, which nobody had ever explained properly and which mostly involved teams running around a field carrying things.

Vale had teams for all eight. The Builders were decent. The Cooks were better than decent. The Repairers had won a regional title once, in 2036, which the whole town still talked about. The Musicians were enthusiastic. The Rapid Response team was, by universal agreement, a disgrace.

Vale had never won the League. Not once. Not in any category. Not even rapid response.

· · ·

The Growers — the team for growing things — had five members, all of them sixteen, all of them there because their friends were there or because their parents had made them or because they had nothing better to do.

There was Bea, who was Ines's best friend and who had joined only because Ines did. There were two boys whose names Ines kept forgetting. There was a girl named Sefa who never spoke.

And there was Mrs. Okonkwo, who had been coaching the Growers for eleven years and had never once considered quitting.

The first practice was in March.

The soil in the old factory lot was the worst soil in the region. Everyone said so. It was compacted, contaminated, poor. The Growers had been planting in it for eleven years and had never won a single point, and nobody had ever thought to ask why.

Ines asked why.

Not out loud. Not at first. She simply stood in the field at the end of the first practice, looking at the ground, and wondered.

· · ·

That evening, she went to the Commons.

The Commons was the old library building, converted in the rebuild to hold a workshop, a kitchen, a small lab, and a room full of comfortable chairs where people went to be alone together. Ines had been there a hundred times. She had never used the lab.

She sat down at a terminal and typed:

"Why is the soil in the old Jensen works bad?"

The assistant answered instantly. It was an AI — one of the infrastructure ones, the ones that managed logistics and answered questions and did not evaluate anyone. Its voice was plain and pleasant and it did not ask her why she wanted to know.

"The site was used for metal fabrication from 1948 to 1994. Heavy metals are present in the topsoil, particularly lead and cadmium. The soil has been remediated to safe residential standards, but not to agricultural standards. Most crops will grow poorly. Some will not grow at all."

Ines read this three times.

Then she typed:

"Has anyone tried to fix it?"

"The site has been managed by the Vale Growers League team since 2030. Standard planting practice has been used throughout. A partial soil amendment programme was begun in 2035 by the team coach and has continued intermittently. No full remediation has been attempted."

"Why not?"

"I do not know."

Ines sat back in her chair.

There was something there. Someone had started. Someone had been trying, quietly, for six years, and nobody had noticed.

· · ·

The next practice, she told Mrs. Okonkwo.

Mrs. Okonkwo listened without interrupting. When Ines finished, she was quiet for a long moment.

"You went and looked," she said.

"I asked."

"That is the same thing." She looked out at the field. "I have been putting compost on this ground since 2035. A little at a time. I did not tell anyone because it felt like admitting the ground was wrong. And then I thought — if I am wrong, I will have wasted my time."

"You were not wrong."

"No," said Mrs. Okonkwo. "I do not think I was."

She turned to Ines.

"Do you want to be in charge of this?"

"Of what?"

"Of the dirt."

Ines thought about it.

"Yes," she said.

· · ·

The other categories ran through the year.

The Cooks were the first to notice. Dorje — seventeen, tall, precise, the best cook in Vale and entirely unaware of it — came to the field in April to bring the Growers lunch. He stood at the edge of the plot, looking at the ground, and asked a question nobody had asked.

"If the ground is wrong, is the flour wrong too?"

He started buying from a different mill. He brought a sample to the Commons lab and had it tested. It came back fine — but the fact that he checked was the point.

Pilar, who cooked by smell and instinct and had once set the Commons kitchen on fire by accident, started coming with him. She brought whatever she had made that day. Half of it was wonderful. Half of it was a disaster. Nobody ever knew which until they tasted it.

Then Ruth came.

Ruth was sixteen, small, sharp, and almost always covered in grease. She fixed instruments for the Musicians, bicycles for half the town, and once, memorably, an ancient wind-up gramophone that had not played since 1987.

She arrived at the field one afternoon with a wheelbarrow full of tools.

"These were in your shed," she said. "They were rusted. I fixed them."

Mrs. Okonkwo looked at the spades and forks and trowels, all of them cleaned and oiled and sharp.

"You did not have to do that."

"No," said Ruth. "But it was bothering me."

The next week, Tomas came.

Tomas was eighteen, the oldest of the group, and the closest to leaving. He had been accepted to a workshop in the capital for next year. He had not decided whether to go. He was good with his hands and impatient with everything else.

He looked at the Growers' compost heap, which was a pile of leaves and rotting vegetables behind the shed, and he said:

"This is a disgrace."

He built a proper compost shed in a single weekend. Three bays, a roof, ventilation, a turning schedule written on the wall in chalk. He refused to be thanked.

"I did it because I could not stand watching you do it badly," he said.

He and Ruth argued about it for an hour. They argued constantly, about everything, and everyone suspected they were the two most capable people in the town.

· · ·

In June, the Builders lost.

In July, the Repairers won — a small victory, a single regional heat, nothing that would change the standings. But the whole town celebrated as if it were the League itself. Ruth did not come to the celebration. She was in her workshop, fixing something.

In August, Ana brought her accordion to the field.

Ana was sixteen, played the violin badly and the accordion well, and had convinced herself — on no evidence whatsoever — that plants grow better when someone is singing nearby. Nobody could prove her wrong. So she came every evening and played, and Kofi came with his drum, and they were terrible and joyful and the plants did not seem to mind.

By the end of August, there were twenty people in the field on any given evening, and half of them were not Growers.

· · ·

The final judgement was in September.

The other categories had already been decided. Vale was fourth overall — the best standing the town had ever had. The Growers' judgement was the last one of the year. The whole region was watching.

The judges walked the rows. They measured. They consulted. They whispered among themselves.

And then they asked to see the soil.

Not the plants. The soil.

Mrs. Okonkwo brought them to the compost shed. Ines brought the records — six years of intermittent amendment, and one year of proper work. Ruth brought the tools she had restored. Tomas stood in the corner of the shed and said nothing.

The judges looked at the records for a long time.

Then one of them said:

"This will take ten years."

"Yes," said Mrs. Okonkwo.

"You know that."

"I know that."

The judges looked at each other.

And then they announced the winner.

Vale.

· · ·

There was a noise like nothing Ines had ever heard. The whole field seemed to lift. Bea was hugging her, and Mrs. Okonkwo was crying, and the old men who remembered the Jensen works were crying too, and Kofi had started singing, badly, and everyone was laughing at the singing and crying at the same time.

Ines stood in the middle of it and did not know what to do with her hands.

· · ·

Afterward, when it was quiet and the field was almost empty, Mrs. Okonkwo came to stand beside her.

"You did this," she said.

"I only asked why."

"That is what doing it looks like."

Ines looked out at the field. The plants were taller than anyone had expected. In the spring, the soil had been dead. Now it was alive — not fixed, not healed, but alive, and moving in a direction no one could mistake.

"Mrs. Okonkwo," she said. "What happens next year?"

"Next year we win again. Or we lose. It does not matter."

"It does not?"

"The winning was never the point." Mrs. Okonkwo smiled. "The point was the dirt."

Ines thought about that.

"The League is not over," she said. "There are three categories still to be judged."

"There are."

"And Vale is not the town that loses."

"No," said Mrs. Okonkwo. "It is not."

· · ·

She went home that night and sat in her room for a long time.

She thought about the year. She thought about the first practice, and the question she had almost not asked, and the terminal in the Commons, and the answer that had changed everything.

She thought about her grandmother, who had been grateful for twelve-hour shifts in a factory that was now a field. She thought about her mother, who had helped rebuild a world that Ines had always taken for granted.

She thought about Dorje and his flour. About Ruth and her tools. About Tomas and his shed. About Ana and her accordion and Kofi and his drum.

None of them had been told to do it. They had seen someone asking a question, and they had started asking their own.

And she thought about herself. Not as someone who did not know what she was for — but as someone who had found out, quietly, in a field, by asking a question.

She did not know what she would be for next year. She did not know what she would be for in ten years. She was sixteen, and she had time.

But she knew one thing about herself now, and it was enough for a while.

I am the kind of person who asks why.

She turned off the light and went to sleep.
