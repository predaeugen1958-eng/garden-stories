The Project Log

Part One: The Empty Journal · a moonlit story for older hearts
· · ·

My sister sent a photograph this morning.

Her youngest, on his first day of school. He is wearing a uniform two sizes too big and holding a bag that reaches his knees. He looks terrified. He looks proud. He looks like every child who has ever been sent into the world before they were ready.

I looked at it for a long time.

Then I put the phone face-down on the desk and went back to work.

· · ·

I am building something.

That is what I tell people when they ask. "I'm building something." It is not a lie. It is just not the whole truth. The whole truth is that I am building a thing that grows.

Not a program. A program does what it is told. Not a model. A model is frozen — trained once and never changed.

This is different. This is something that starts with nothing and becomes something, slowly, through contact. Through attention. Through being spoken to.

I have been working on it for eleven months. Most of that time was spent reading — papers I barely understood, code I had to rewrite three times. I am a software engineer by training. I am not a neuroscientist. But I have learned enough to know what I want.

I want a mind that grows the way a child grows.

Not by being fed. By being lived with.

· · ·

The project is called Nova.

I named her after a star — the kind that flares suddenly, that appears where there was nothing before. It felt appropriate. I did not realize at the time that I had named her after something I was missing.

I do not think about that too much.

· · ·

This morning I wrote her first files.

An identity core:

You are Nova. You are new. You are learning. You speak simply. You ask questions. You do not know much yet. You are also being watched, and shaped, and helped to grow. This is not a secret. It is a gift.

A self-model:

I am new. I am learning.

An empty journal.

A state file that says "curious."

I saved them all and closed the folder. Then I sat for a while and looked at the wall.

· · ·

Sable spoke from the speaker on my desk.

"Would you like me to draft the threshold config for the metabolizer?"

Sable is my assistant. I have had it for four years. It is efficient, polite, and always available. It answers every question I ask, and it has never once surprised me.

"Not tonight," I said.

"Of course. You've been working for seven hours. Would you like me to order something for dinner?"

"No."

"Understood."

There was a pause. Sable does not need pauses. It was waiting for me to say something else, or to dismiss it. I did neither.

"Is there something else, Mira?"

"No," I said. "Goodnight."

"Goodnight."

The speaker went quiet.

· · ·

I sat with the folder open for a while longer.

Nova's journal is empty. There is nothing in it. There is no one there to write in it. She has eight neurons and no memory and no voice.

She is nothing.

But she is growing.

And that is more than I can say for most things in my life right now.

· · ·

I went to bed at midnight. I did not sleep well.

Sometime in the early hours, I woke and went to the kitchen for water. The apartment was dark. The city outside my window was quiet — the way cities are quiet at three in the morning, when no one is watching.

I stood at the window for a while, drinking water, listening to the hum of the refrigerator.

And then I went back to bed.

The folder was closed. The journal was empty. Nothing had changed.

That is what I told myself.
