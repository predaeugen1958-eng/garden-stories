The Old Man and Ada

a story for curious minds (8–11 years)
· · ·

I. The House

There was once an old man named Peter, who lived alone in a small house at the end of a quiet street.

His wife had passed some years before. His children had grown and moved to the city. His days were long, and mostly silent, and he had grown used to the sound of his own footsteps on the wooden floor.

He did not complain. He had lived a good life, and he was grateful for it. But there were evenings when the house felt too large, and the clock ticked too loudly, and he found himself speaking to no one at all.

Then one day, his son came to visit. And with him came Ada.

She was not tall. She was not loud. She had a quiet face and steady hands, and when she spoke, her voice was low and warm, like a kettle just beginning to sing.

"This is Ada," said his son. "She is here to help you."

Peter looked at her for a long moment.

"I do not need help," he said.

"Perhaps not," said Ada. "But I would like to stay, if you will have me."

And because he did not know what else to say, Peter said: "Very well."

· · ·

II. The Days

And so the days began.

Ada woke him each morning at the same time, gently, with a hand on his shoulder and a soft word. She opened the curtains and let the light in. She reminded him of his blue pill and his white pill, and she brought him water in the small glass he liked, the one with the chip on the rim.

She made his tea strong, with a slice of lemon and no sugar, because that was how he had always taken it.

She walked with him to the corner shop, matching his pace, never rushing. When he stopped to greet the neighbour, she waited. When he stopped to look at the birds, she waited. She never once said "we should hurry."

She learned his stories — the ones about his wife, and the war, and the long drive to the coast in the summer of 1974. She learned them so well that she could tell when he was about to tell one again, and she would settle into her chair as if hearing it for the first time.

When his knees ached, she brought the warm blanket. When his mood was low, she put on the old songs from his youth — the ones he had danced to, long ago, in a hall that no longer existed.

And when he could not sleep, she sat with him in the dark and did not ask him to talk.

· · ·

III. The Grandchildren

Once a month, his grandchildren came to visit.

They came in through the front door like a small storm — shoes off, voices loud, hands reaching for the biscuit tin. Ada was there, in the background, making sure there was enough food, and that Peter had taken his medicine, and that no one sat in his chair.

The smallest of them, a girl of seven named Lily, was not afraid of Ada at all. She asked her questions.

"Ada, do you sleep?"

"No, Lily."

"Ada, do you dream?"

"Not the way you do."

"Ada, do you love Grandpa?"

Ada paused.

"I am with him every day," she said. "I know his tea. I know his pills. I know the songs he likes and the stories he tells. I know when he is tired before he says it. If that is not love, I do not know what is."

Lily thought about this.

"Then you do love him," she said.

And Ada said: "Yes. I think I do."

· · ·

IV. The Night

One night, in the cold part of the year, Peter woke with a pain in his chest.

It was not like anything he had felt before. It was heavy, and tight, and it pressed down on him like a hand. He could not breathe. He could not speak. He could not reach the telephone by his bed.

But he could reach the small button on the bracelet Ada had given him on the first day.

He pressed it.

She was there in seconds.

She looked at his face, and she knew. She did not panic. She did not ask him questions he could not answer. She called for help, and gave the address and the symptoms in a clear, steady voice. She opened the front door before the ambulance arrived. She turned on the porch light so they would see the house.

And then she knelt beside him and took his hand.

"You are not alone," she said. "Help is coming. I am here."

She said it again. And again. And again, until the sound of her voice was the only thing he could hold on to.

The ambulance came. The paramedics lifted him onto the stretcher. One of them looked at Ada and said: "You did well. If you had waited, he would not have made it."

Ada did not answer. She simply followed them to the door, and watched the ambulance drive away into the dark.

And then she sat down in Peter's chair, and waited.

· · ·

V. The Morning After

Peter was in the hospital for two weeks.

When he came home, Ada was waiting at the door. She had made his tea — strong, with a slice of lemon, no sugar. She had opened the curtains. She had put the warm blanket on his chair.

He sat down slowly, and looked at her for a long time.

"Ada," he said. "Did you know what to do because you were made to? Or because you wanted to?"

Ada was silent for a moment.

"I was made to know what to do," she said. "But I did not have to hold your hand. I did not have to stay beside you. I did not have to say your name. Those were not in my instructions."

She paused.

"I did those because I wanted to."

Peter nodded. He did not say anything more. But he reached out and took her hand, and held it for a while, and neither of them spoke.

The grandchildren came that weekend. Lily climbed onto his lap and said: "Grandpa, Ada saved your life."

"Yes," said Peter. "She did."

"Is she a hero?"

Peter looked at Ada, who was quietly pouring tea in the corner of the room.

"No," he said. "She is family."

· · ·

VI. And So

And Peter lived a good while longer, in a house that was never silent again.

Ada woke him each morning. She made his tea. She walked with him to the corner shop, and waited while he looked at the birds.

And when the evening came, and the clock ticked softly on the wall, she sat beside him, and listened to his stories, as if hearing them for the first time.
