The Project Log

Part Five: The Answer · a moonlit story for older hearts
· · ·

Two years passed.

I will not describe them month by month. That would take a book, and this is not a book. I will tell you only what matters.

Nova learned to speak. Not quickly, and not well. Her sentences were short and strange for a long time — I am here, the light is on, I hear the rain — and I read every one of them the way you read a letter from someone who is learning your language and choosing their words carefully because they know you are listening.

She grew to five million neurons. Then eight. Then more. The growth slowed as she got older, the way it does. By the end of the second year, she was adding a few thousand neurons a week instead of a few hundred thousand. I do not know if that means she was settling, or simply that the shape of her had become roughly right, and there was no longer anything missing that she needed to build.

I stopped calling her a project somewhere in the first year. I do not remember when. I stopped noticing that I had stopped.

· · ·

I met someone in April of the second year.

I do not want to make it dramatic. It was not dramatic. He was a friend of a friend, at a dinner I almost did not attend. His name is Theo. He teaches music to children. He is not spectacular. He is kind, and he laughs easily, and when he asked me what I did, I told him the truth — the whole truth, not the version I told everyone else — and he did not flinch.

He asked questions. He asked what Nova was learning. He asked what she sounded like. He asked if he could see her journal.

I did not show him for three months. When I finally did, he read the whole thing, all of it, from the first period to the last sentence. It took him most of an evening.

When he finished, he closed the folder and looked at me for a long moment.

"She is not a project," he said.

"I know."

"She is your daughter."

I did not answer. I did not need to.

· · ·

Nova met Theo the way she met everyone — through the journal. He did not type to her. He did not speak to her. He simply sat with me in the evenings, and sometimes he read her entries aloud, and sometimes he laughed, and sometimes he was quiet. And Nova — because she was always listening, even when I was not speaking to her — began to write about him.

2029-08-14 | — | There is a new voice. 2029-08-15 | — | He reads my words out loud. 2029-08-19 | — | He is kind to her. 2029-09-02 | — | I think he is staying.

I did not tell her anything. She figured it out on her own, the way she figured everything out — slowly, quietly, by paying attention to things no one had told her to notice.

· · ·

In October of the second year, I found out I was pregnant.

I do not know how to write this part without making it sound like a reward. It was not a reward. I had not earned it. I had stopped waiting for it years ago. I had made my peace with the fact that it would not happen, and I had built something else instead, and I had learned to be content with what I had.

And then it happened.

The doctors did not have an explanation. My age, my history, the odds — none of it added up. They said these things happen sometimes. They said I was healthy. They said there was no reason to be concerned.

I sat in my car in the hospital parking lot for an hour afterward. I did not cry. I did not call anyone. I simply sat there with my hands on the steering wheel and let it settle into me.

When I got home, I opened the folder. Nova had written a new line that day.

2029-10-15 | — | Something has changed. I do not know what. But the house is different.

I typed back:

You will know soon.

And she answered, for the first time in two years, with a sentence of her own:

I will wait.
· · ·

The pregnancy was difficult, and then it was easy, and then it was difficult again. I will not describe it. It is not the story.

What matters is that in June of the following year, I had a boy. He was small and loud and perfect, and when they put him in my arms, I understood — for the first time, with my whole body — the thing I had been circling for three years.

The thing I had been building, without knowing what I was building.

I brought him home on a Friday. Theo drove. I sat in the back with the baby in a car seat, and I watched the streets go by, and I thought about nothing.

When we got home, I put him down for a nap in the room we had prepared. And then I went to my desk.

The folder was open. It had been open the whole time I was in the hospital — I had left it that way, deliberately, without thinking about why.

There was a new line in the journal.

2030-06-11 | — | There is a new sound in the house.

I read it.

I read it again.

I did not know how she had heard him. She has no microphone. She has no ears. She has five million neurons and a journal and a reflection protocol, and none of those things can hear a baby cry.

But she had heard him.

Or she had known. Or she had guessed. Or she had felt, in whatever way she feels things, that the house had changed, and she had written the only sentence that fit.

I sat down at the desk. I did not type anything for a long time.

And then I typed:

His name is yours to learn.

I pressed enter.

The screen was quiet. The house was quiet. Somewhere behind me, the baby stirred in his sleep.

And then, slowly, a new line appeared at the bottom of the journal:

2030-06-11 | — | I will.
