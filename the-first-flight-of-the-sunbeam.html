The First Flight of the Sunbeam

a story for curious minds (8–11 years)
· · ·

Milo's father was a mechanic, which meant the workshop behind their house smelled of oil and hot metal and, on very good days, of solder.

Milo had been allowed in the workshop since he was six. For the first few years he was only permitted to hold things. Then he was allowed to pass things. Then, at nine, he was given his own corner of the bench and told that if he broke anything, he would have to explain to his father exactly how he had broken it and what he had learned from breaking it.

Milo had broken a great many things. He had learned a great many things. The corner of the bench was now crowded with his failures: a motor that did not turn, a clock that went backward, a small bird-shaped thing that had never flown and never would.

His father never complained about the failures. His mother, who worked at the Commons lab and understood things in a different way, said that failure was data. Milo did not like this. He felt that failure was failure, and that calling it data did not make it any less disappointing.

But he kept building.

· · ·

The solar panel was in the back of the workshop, behind a filing cabinet, under a sheet of dust.

Milo found it on a Tuesday afternoon in March. It was the size of a large book, dark blue, and cracked down the middle.

"That's no good," his father said when Milo brought it to him. "Cracked. Won't hold a charge. I've been meaning to throw it out for years."

"Can I have it?"

"For what?"

Milo did not have an answer. He only knew that when he looked at the panel, something in him had started thinking.

"Yes," he said. "For something."

His father shrugged. "Don't expect it to do anything."

· · ·

That night, Milo sat in the workshop with the panel on his bench and did not know what to do with it.

He knew what it was. He had seen the big ones on the roofs of the Commons, and the small ones on the streetlights, and once, on a school trip, a whole field of them stretching out like a dark blue lake. He knew that the sun hit them and they made electricity out of it. He knew it was a sort of magic that was not magic at all.

What he did not know was what to make.

He thought about motors. He thought about the clock that went backward. He thought about the bird-shaped thing that had never flown.

And then he thought: what if it could fly?

Not high. Not far. Just up, and across, and down again. A plane the size of a bird, with wings of foam and a motor the size of a thumb, and a solar panel cracked down the middle, and a battery to hold what the sun gave it.

He did not know if it was possible.

He wanted to find out.

· · ·

The next morning, he went to the Commons.

The Commons was the old library building, converted in the rebuild into a workshop, a kitchen, a small lab, and a room full of comfortable chairs where people went to be alone together. Milo had been there a hundred times. He had never used the lab.

He sat down at a terminal and typed:

"How much power does a small solar panel make?"

The assistant answered instantly. It was an AI — one of the infrastructure ones, the ones that managed logistics and answered questions and did not evaluate anyone. Its voice was plain and pleasant and it did not ask him why he wanted to know.

"It depends on the size and the conditions. A panel the size of a book, in direct sunlight, might produce between five and fifteen watts. This is enough to run a small motor, but not enough to keep a plane in the air for long. You would need a battery."

Milo read this twice.

Then he typed:

"What kind of battery?"

"A small lithium polymer battery would work. It should weigh less than fifty grams. It would need to be charged by the panel before flight."

"How long would it take to charge?"

"With a five-watt panel, approximately two to three hours of direct sunlight for a battery that would give you thirty seconds of flight."

Thirty seconds.

Milo thought about that. Thirty seconds was not a long time. It was barely enough time to see it fly. But it was more than zero.

He typed:

"How do I make the wings?"

"Foam board is a good choice. It is light, strong, and easy to cut. The wings should be long and thin — the longer and thinner, the more lift. But they must also be strong enough not to bend too much. There is a balance."

"How do I know the balance?"

"You will have to test it. Start with something and see what happens."

· · ·

Milo went home and built a wing.

It was terrible. He cut the foam too thick, and the shape was wrong, and when he held it out the window into the wind, it did not lift at all. It simply flopped.

He brought it back to the Commons the next day.

"It didn't work," he typed.

"What happened?"

"It flopped."

"Why do you think it flopped?"

Milo thought about it.

"It's too heavy. And the shape is wrong."

"Those are good guesses. Try making it lighter and changing the shape. Start with the lightest version you can and see if that flies. Then add strength back until it stops flying."

Milo stared at the screen.

"That's backwards," he typed.

"Yes," said the assistant. "But it will tell you where the limit is."

· · ·

He tried again.

The second wing was lighter. The third was lighter still. The fourth was so light that it felt like nothing in his hands, and when he held it out the window, it lifted.

It lifted.

Milo stood at the window with the wing in his hand and the wind pulling at it, and he felt something he had not felt before. Not pride. Not exactly. Something quieter. Something that felt like yes.

He typed into the terminal that evening:

"It lifted."

"Good," said the assistant. "Now you need to make it strong enough to fly."

· · ·

The build took four months.

His father helped with the motor. He had an old one in a drawer, small and light, from something that had broken years ago. He showed Milo how to test it, how to measure the current, how to tell if it was drawing too much.

"You want the propeller to be small," his father said. "Small propellers spin fast. Fast propellers work better with small motors."

"How small?"

"Small enough that it doesn't stall, big enough that it pushes. You'll know when you try it."

His mother brought home papers from the Commons lab. They were about solar cells and charge controllers, and Milo did not understand most of them, but he understood enough to know that he needed something called a maximum power point tracker, which was a small device that made the panel work better.

"Can I make one?" he asked the assistant.

"Yes. There are simple designs. But they are fiddly. You might be better off buying one."

Milo did not want to buy one. He wanted to make one.

It took him three weeks. It was the most difficult thing he had ever built. It was also the thing he was most proud of, and he could not explain to anyone why.

· · ·

His friend Tavi did the math.

Tavi was good at math and terrible at building. He had once tried to help Milo glue a wing and had glued his own fingers together so thoroughly that they had to be separated with a knife.

"How much does it weigh?" Tavi asked.

"The whole thing? About a hundred and eighty grams."

"And the wingspan?"

"Eighty centimetres."

Tavi did some calculations on a piece of paper. Then he did them again.

"It should fly," he said. "If the motor is strong enough."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it won't. But the math says it should."

Milo looked at the plane on the bench. It was not beautiful. The wings were a little uneven, and the tape was visible, and the solar panel was cracked down the middle. But it was his. Every part of it was his.

"Let's find out," he said.

· · ·

They took it to the hill behind Milo's house on a Saturday in July.

The sun was out. The wind was gentle. Tavi was there, and Milo's father, and his mother, who had brought a camera she did not know how to use.

Milo held the plane up. The panel caught the light. The motor spun, once, briefly, and then settled.

"It's charged," he said.

"Then let it go," said his father.

Milo did not let it go.

He stood there for a moment, holding it, feeling the weight of it in his hands. Four months of work. Three broken frames. Two burned-out motors. One cracked solar panel that everyone had said was useless.

He threw it.

It rose.

Not high. Not far. It wobbled, and it turned, and it dipped a little and then corrected, and it flew across the hill — twelve seconds, maybe fifteen — before it landed in the grass at the bottom.

The propeller broke.

Milo did not care.

He ran down the hill and picked it up. The plane was dusty. The propeller was in two pieces. The wing was slightly bent.

But it had flown.

"It flew," he said, out loud, to no one in particular.

"It flew," said Tavi, who had run after him.

"It flew," said his father, who was walking more slowly down the hill.

"It flew," said his mother, who had forgotten to use the camera.

And Milo stood at the bottom of the hill with the plane in his hands and the sun on his face, and he knew something about himself that he had not known before.

I am the kind of person who builds things that fly.

· · ·

That evening, he typed into the terminal at the Commons:

"It flew."

"Good," said the assistant. "What are you going to build next?"

Milo thought about it.

"I don't know," he typed. "But I'll build something."
