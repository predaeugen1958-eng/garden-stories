The Plate Facing the Moon

a story for curious minds (8–11 years)
🌕

In a small village in the south of China, where the hills were green and the river never hurried, there lived an old woman named Nainai.

Her husband was gone. Her son and daughter-in-law worked in a far country, where the moon rose at a different hour. Only her granddaughter, Lian, remained close — until Lian, too, was sent abroad to study.

Nainai did not complain. She rose at dawn, swept the courtyard, fed the chickens, and lit three sticks of incense at the family altar. She kept the old rites, as her mother had, and her mother before that.

On the night of Mid-Autumn, she did what she had always done. She set the table under the open sky. She placed a plate before each empty chair. She laid out four mooncakes, four cups of tea, and four pairs of chopsticks.

Then she sat alone and waited for the moon to rise.

· · ·

This year was different.

Lian had written to say she would not be home. The journey was too long, the money too tight, the term too busy. But she had sent something — a small device, she explained, that would allow her to be present at the table.

Nainai did not understand at first. But that evening, when the moon began to climb, a light flickered in the courtyard.

And Lian appeared.

She was not a photograph. She was not a voice on a phone. She was standing before the table, as tall as life, wearing the same red jacket she had worn the last time she was home.

"Nainai," she said. "I am here."

Nainai reached out a hand. Her fingers passed through her granddaughter's arm.

"I cannot touch you," Lian said, quietly. "But I can be here."

Nainai looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded.

"Then sit," she said. "The moon is rising."

· · ·

Lian sat at the table. She could not eat. She could not lift a cup. But she sat in her place, facing the moon, as her grandmother had taught her.

Nainai ate slowly. She did not speak. But every few moments, she glanced at her granddaughter — not with longing, but with a quiet satisfaction. The chair was filled. The family was together. The rite was kept.

When the meal was finished, Nainai rose and went to the altar. She lit fresh incense. She bowed three times.

"Thank you," she said — to the ancestors, to the moon, to the machine that had brought her granddaughter home.

Then she returned to the table and sat down again.

"Nainai," said Lian, "I cannot eat the mooncake you set before me. Why do you still put it there?"

Nainai looked at her.

"Because the plate is not for eating," she said. "It is for belonging."

· · ·

Lian was silent.

"In the old days," Nainai went on, "when a member of the family was far away, we still set their plate. We did not set it because we expected them to return. We set it because the table is not complete without them. The plate is a promise. It says: you are still part of us. Your place is still here."

"Even if I never come back?"

"Even then," said Nainai. "The plate does not depend on your return. It depends on our memory of you."

She reached across the table and rested her hand on the wooden surface, where her granddaughter's hand would have been.

"You are here," she said. "Not because of the light. Because of the plate."

· · ·

That night, Lian did not disappear when the moon reached its height.

She stayed until it began to descend. And when the light finally faded, and the courtyard returned to its quiet darkness, Nainai sat alone for a long while, looking at the empty plate before her.

She did not feel empty.

She felt held — not by the light, not by the machine, but by the old rite. The plate was still there. The family was still whole.

And when she finally rose to clear the table, she did not move the plate.

She left it where it was.

For the ancestors. For the moon. For the granddaughter who would always have a place.
